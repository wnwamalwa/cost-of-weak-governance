What does weak governance cost Kenya?

Corruption and weak accountability widen the budget deficit, raise power and transport costs, hold back firms, and tax the earnings of informal workers. This dashboard puts published estimates from the World Bank and Kenya’s own watchdogs in shillings, jobs and debt.

Where the number comes from

It is a World Bank estimate published in the Kenya Economic Update No. 32 (2025) and repeated in Edition 34 (November 2026, note 91). Bribes are hidden and cannot be counted directly, so this is an estimate of the total, not a sum of recorded payments.

Who pays it

Mostly informal transport workers, such as boda boda riders and matatu crews, and small traders. The World Bank reports bribe demands from traffic police, inspection services and county agencies of KSh 50–100 a day, 5–20% of daily earnings, plus occasional larger demands. Informal workers are 83.8% of Kenya’s workforce.

How big is it?

  • About 0.5% of GDP (KSh 88 bn of about KSh 18.9 trillion).
  • KSh 241 million a day, or about KSh 1,600 per Kenyan a year.
  • 26 times the KSh 3.4 bn the EACC recovered in FY2024/25.

Why it matters

It is money taken straight out of take-home pay, on top of fuel taxes and county and SACCO fees. It raises fares and prices, makes daily income uncertain, and falls hardest on the poorest workers.

How to read it

This is an estimate, not audited transactions. It is also a different kind of cost from the KSh 380 bn headline: bribes move money from citizens to officials and do not appear in the budget, while the headline measures the extra budget deficit. The two should not be added together.

About this dashboard

The Cost of Weak Governance dashboard shows what corruption and weak accountability cost Kenya: in a wider budget deficit and more debt, higher power and transport costs, firms that invest and innovate less, and bribes taken from the earnings of informal workers. It brings together the World Bank’s November 2026 Kenya Economic Update and the latest reports from Kenya’s own oversight bodies.

How to read this dashboard

  • The headline converts the World Bank’s estimate (governance constraints widen the deficit by about 2% of GDP a year) into shillings at the latest nominal GDP. It is an order of magnitude, not an audited loss.
  • The channel figures are not added together, to avoid double counting.
  • The ticker spreads the annual estimate evenly over the year.
  • Debt, GDP and population come live from the Kenya National Debt Counter when it can be reached; otherwise the page uses the last saved figures.
  • The Africa map uses the World Bank’s Control of Corruption score (about −2.5 worst to +2.5 best), the index behind the report’s reform scenarios.
